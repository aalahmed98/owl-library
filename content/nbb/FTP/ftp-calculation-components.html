Anatomy of an FTP Calculation

Every component of the rate, illustrated · Companion to the FTP Reference Guide · 2026

An FTP rate looks like a single number — "this loan is charged 5.15%" — but that number is assembled from distinct parts, each answering a different question. This paper takes the calculation apart: what each component is, where its number comes from, and how the pieces are put back together for a real deal. The running example throughout is a 5-year corporate loan.

Component 1 — The base rate: read, not calculated

The foundation of every FTP rate is the market yield curve — usually the swap or OIS curve. Nothing about it is bank-specific: it is the price at which money itself trades, observed from real instruments at every tenor. The only "calculation" is a lookup: find the deal's tenor on the x-axis, read the rate off the y-axis.

The one rule that matters is matched tenor: a 5-year deal reads the 5-year point, never the overnight rate plus hope. Matched-tenor pricing is what makes the rest of the framework honest — it hands each deal the funding cost of the horizon it actually occupies.

Component 2 — The term liquidity premium: the price of our name

The swap curve prices money in the abstract. But this bank is not an abstraction — it borrows at a spread above that curve, and the spread grows with tenor: lenders who tie their money up in one name for five years demand more compensation than lenders parked overnight. That spread, read at the deal's tenor, is the term liquidity premium.

Two properties matter. It is bank-specific — a strongly rated bank pays a few tens of basis points, a stressed one pays hundreds. And it is the volatile component: the base curve moves with monetary policy at a measured pace, but the premium can triple in a week when confidence cracks. In 2008 and March 2020 it was the premium, not the base rate, that repriced every balance sheet in the market.

Component 3 — Add-ons: the price of the fine print

The last block is a set of smaller charges for features of the specific deal that create funding risk beyond its plain tenor:

  • Contingent liquidity — an undrawn credit line obliges the bank to hold standby liquidity for money the customer may never take. The buffer isn't free, so the commitment carries a charge.
  • Basis / cross-currency — a deal funded in one currency or index but earning in another leaves a mismatch that must be hedged; the hedge cost is passed to the deal.
  • Optionality — a borrower's right to prepay (or a depositor's to withdraw early) is an option the bank has sold. Options have value; the add-on collects it upfront.

Which tenor? — Bucketing decides where you read the curve

Components 1 and 2 are both read at a tenor — so the bucketing decision, which tenor a deal is assigned, silently controls both numbers. Three rules cover every balance:

Balance typeTenor usedWhy
Fixed-rate, contractual maturity (bullet loan, term deposit)Contractual maturityThe cash flow date is written in the contract — mechanical.
Floating-rateRepricing maturity (the reset period)The rate re-fixes at every reset; funding risk only runs to the next reset.
Non-maturity (current/savings accounts)Behavioural tranches (core / volatile / surge)No contractual date exists; observed stability decides the mix of buckets.

Component 4 — Cash-flow weighting: blended FTP and average life

Everything so far prices one cash flow at one tenor. Real deals return principal on a schedule, and each slice of that schedule is its own mini-deal, priced at its own bucket. The single rate quoted on the deal is the principal-weighted average across all slices:

Blended FTP = Σ (cash flow in year t × curve rate at t) ÷ Σ (total cash flow)
Average life = Σ (cash flow in year t × t) ÷ Σ (total cash flow)

Average life is the same weighting applied to time instead of rate — it says which single tenor the schedule behaves like. A 5-year equal-amortising loan has an average life of 3 years: it is, economically, a 3-year deal wearing a 5-year contract.
