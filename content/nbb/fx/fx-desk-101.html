The FX Desk: The 101

Spot, forwards, swaps, and how the desk actually earns — expanded from desk notes · Part of the Treasury series: FTP · money markets · 2026

Start with one client, one story

A client in Turkey holds dollars and needs Bahraini dinar. The sequence:

  1. The client instructs a transfer of USD from their Turkish account to their Bahrain account.
  2. The bank confirms the trade: what is being exchanged, at what rate, and for which value date. If the agreement is "today's rate, money lands tomorrow", then the rate is fixed today even though the cash arrives tomorrow — the confirmation is what makes that binding on both sides.
  3. The dollars land, the bank converts at the agreed rate, and BHD is credited.

Bid, ask, and where the desk's money comes from

The desk quotes every pair two-sided, e.g. 2.553 / 2.554:

  • Bid = 2.553 — the rate at which the bank buys the base currency from you.
  • Ask = 2.554 — the rate at which the bank sells the base currency to you.

So the customer holding euros who wants BHD sells their euros to the bank — they deal at the bid, the lower rate. A different customer who wants euros buys them from the bank at the ask. The bank buys low, sells high, and the 0.001 between the two is the spread — the desk's basic income. One careful note: the bank never needs the rate to move to earn this; it earns by standing in the middle of two-way flow.

Value dates — "spot" is not one thing

Even a plain spot trade settles on a chosen value date: value today, value tomorrow (tom), or T+2 — the standard spot convention. The rate is agreed at confirmation regardless; only the settlement day changes (and the price adjusts by a whisker of interest for each day of difference).

Forwards — premium, discount, and why it's arithmetic, not opinion

A forward rate is not the desk's forecast of where the currency is going. It comes from the interest rates of the two currencies, mechanically:

Forward = Spot × (1 + rquote × t) ÷ (1 + rbase × t)

Intuition: holding the high-interest currency for three months earns you more interest, so the forward price must give that advantage back — otherwise everyone would hold the high-yielder risk-free. When the forward is above spot the base currency trades at a premium; below spot, at a discount. The difference, quoted in points, is the forward points.

FX swaps — near leg, far leg

An FX swap is two exchanges in one ticket: the near leg (usually spot) swaps currency A for currency B now; the far leg reverses it on a future date at a rate set today — spot plus the forward points. Nobody keeps any currency exposure; what actually happened is that one side borrowed a currency against the other. This is why the money markets paper calls FX swaps a funding tool: for many non-US banks it is a primary dollar funding channel.

Arbitrage — why the numbers can't disagree for long

The spot rate, the forward rate, and the two currencies' interest rates are locked together by the formula above. If the market's forward drifts away from the arithmetic — because someone is quoting lazily, or flows are one-sided — there is a riskless round-trip: borrow one currency, convert at spot, invest at the other rate, sell the proceeds forward, and end with more than you started, with every rate locked on day one. That is covered interest arbitrage. The desk's job is to spot it before everyone else does, because the act of doing the trade closes the gap.

The arbitrage recipe, step by step (forward quoted too high)

Setup: spot 1.1000, EUR rate 2.5%, USD rate 4.5%, 3 months, fair forward 1.1054 — but the market quotes 1.1094.

The forward is rich for EUR, so you want to be a forward seller of EUR:

1 · Borrow $11,000,000 at 4.5% for 3 months — you will owe $11,123,750.
2 · Convert at spot 1.1000 → €10,000,000. Invest at 2.5% → €10,062,500 at maturity.
3 · Today, sell those future euros forward at the rich 1.1094 → locked-in $11,163,338.
4 · At maturity: collect €10,062,500, deliver into the forward, receive $11,163,338, repay the $11,123,750 loan — keep $39,588, with every rate fixed on day one.

If the market forward were too LOW instead, run the loop the other way (borrow EUR, invest USD, buy EUR back cheap forward). The direction follows the sign of the mispricing — the widget picks it for you. Either way, one direction locks a profit whenever the quote and the arithmetic disagree.

The Bahrain angle

USD/BHD is pegged at 0.376 and the Central Bank of Bahrain defends it, so dollar–dinar conversion is near-mechanical — the desk's real trading risk lives in the crosses (EUR, GBP, TRY, JPY against either). For the Turkish client in Figure 1, the interesting leg is not USD→BHD; it's whatever TRY exposure existed before the dollars. And because BHD interest rates shadow US rates to hold the peg (see the FTP guide, section 9), BHD forward points against the dollar stay small and well-behaved — a luxury desks trading unpegged pairs do not have.

Desk vocabulary check — the terms from the notes, one line each: bid/ask: bank buys at bid, sells at ask. Spread: ask − bid, the desk's basic income. Value date: the day cash actually moves (today / tom / T+2 / forward). Confirmation: the binding statement of trade, rate, and value date. Premium / discount: forward above / below spot, set by interest differentials. Near / far leg: the two exchanges inside a swap. Forward points: the spot-to-forward gap, quoted in points. Covered interest arbitrage: the trade that forces spot, forward, and the two interest rates to agree.

Where this sits in Treasury

The FX desk is the third desk in the same room as the money markets desk and the ALM/FTP function. The connections run deep: FX swaps are a funding tool (money markets), the forward points are made of interest differentials (the same curves FTP reads), and client FX flow is priced with a spread exactly the way deposits and loans are priced around the FTP rate — the desk stands in the middle and charges for standing there.
