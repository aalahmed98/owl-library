What Moves FTP

NBB research · Companion to part 3: FTP explained · 2026

The main FTP doc covers the machinery — how the curve splits margins and what to check before publishing it. This one answers a simpler, more fundamental question: where does the FTP number itself come from, what pushes it up and down, and what happens when it moves?

1. The bare bones: FTP is the price of money, for a time, in a place

Strip everything away and the FTP rate is the answer to one question: "what would it cost our bank, today, to borrow this money for this long?" That cost has three layers, and every headline that moves FTP moves one of them:

FTP(tenor) ≈ risk-free rate (what money costs anyone)
+ country & bank risk premium (what lenders charge for us specifically)
+ liquidity premium (the extra for locking money up for the tenor)
  • Layer 1 — the risk-free base. Anchored at the short end by the central bank's policy rate, and further out by where markets expect policy to go. This layer moves on inflation data, central bank meetings, and the economic cycle. It affects every bank in the country identically.
  • Layer 2 — the risk premium. Lenders don't lend to "the market", they lend to a specific bank in a specific country. The riskier either looks, the more they charge. This layer moves on sovereign credit ratings, geopolitics, and the bank's own health — and it can move violently while layer 1 stands still.
  • Layer 3 — the liquidity premium. Money locked up for 5 years must pay more than overnight money, and how much more depends on how nervous markets are. Calm markets: thin premium. Stressed markets: everyone wants their money reachable, so term money gets expensive.

2. The drivers: what pushes FTP up and down

EventFTPWhy
Central bank hikes (inflation fight) UP The risk-free base rises directly; the whole curve lifts, short end first.
Central bank cuts (recession fight) DOWN Mirror image — cheap money policy flows straight into the FTP base.
Country rating downgrade UP Government bond yields jump, and a bank can rarely borrow cheaper than its own government (the "sovereign ceiling"). Every bank in the country pays more for wholesale money, so every FTP curve shifts up — even though the central bank did nothing.
War / geopolitical crisis (in or near the country) UP, sharply Three channels at once: investors demand a bigger risk premium to hold the country's debt (layer 2); depositors and foreign investors pull money out, making funding scarce (layer 3); and the central bank may hike to defend the currency (layer 1). Long tenors rise most — nobody wants to lock money into a war zone for 5 years.
War far away (country is a safe haven) DOWN The reverse flow: money flees the crisis region into safe markets, deposits flood in, funding is abundant, premia compress. The same war raises FTP in one country and lowers it in another.
Financial stress / credit crunch UP Even with policy rates unchanged, banks stop trusting each other, interbank money dries up, and the liquidity premium (layer 3) explodes. 2008's signature: policy rates falling while banks' actual funding cost rose.
The bank's own trouble (losses, downgrade, deposit outflow) UP (that bank only) FTP is bank-specific. A weakened bank pays more for funding than the bank across the street, and its FTP must say so — otherwise it prices loans it can't fund profitably.
Deposit glut (strong inflows, high savings) DOWN (gradually) When cheap, stable funding is abundant, the bank's marginal cost of money falls and the FTP curve can be shaded down, making its loans more competitive.

Notice the pattern: anything that makes lenders more nervous or money more scarce pushes FTP up; anything that makes money cheaper or more abundant pushes it down. FTP is simply the bank's internal thermometer for the temperature of the money around it.

4. What FTP movements do inside the bank

When the FTP curve moves, it propagates through the bank within days:

  • Loan pricing moves. FTP is the largest component of every loan quote (part 2's build-up). FTP up 1% → new loan offers up roughly 1%. Existing fixed-rate loans don't move — which is exactly the mismatch treasury carries and ALM manages.
  • Deposits become more (or less) precious. FTP up means each deposit is credited more internally, so branches suddenly have budget to fight for deposits — you see this on the street as term-deposit campaigns appearing right after rate hikes.
  • Business mix shifts. Products that looked profitable at 3% FTP may not clear their hurdle at 6%; long-tenor lending quietly shrinks when the liquidity premium spikes. FTP is how the bank's balance sheet "feels" the outside world and reshapes itself.
  • Margins get squeezed or fattened with a lag. Funding cost reprices before the loan book does. A fast FTP rise compresses margins first (old loans, new funding costs), then restores them as the book re-prices.

5. What it does to the economy

Zoom out and FTP is the transmission belt of monetary policy. A central bank can only set one overnight rate; it is each bank's FTP system that carries that decision to every car loan, mortgage, and business overdraft in the country:

  • Tightening: policy/risk pushes FTP up → loan rates rise → households and firms borrow less, save more → demand cools → inflation eases. The pain shows up as slower construction, fewer expansions, costlier mortgages.
  • Easing: FTP falls → credit gets cheap → borrowing, investment, and asset prices pick up.
  • The risk-premium channel is the cruel one: after a downgrade or during a conflict, FTP rises without any policy decision — the economy gets a de facto rate hike at the worst possible moment, which is why sovereign stress and credit crunches deepen recessions.
  • Credit rationing: when FTP embeds a big risk premium, marginal borrowers (small firms, first-time buyers) are priced out first — the quantity of credit contracts, not just its price.

So a single sentence to keep: FTP is how the outside world's price of money enters the bank, and how the bank's response exits into the economy. Everything else — the curves, the checklists, the governance in the main FTP doc — is machinery for doing that translation honestly.

6. Steering the bank: the FTP slider

This last section reproduces a hand-drawn treasury sketch (three number lines on an NBB pad, titled "liquidity-based activity") that captures, better than any textbook, how FTP is actually used as a management lever. Picture the deposit rate and the loan rate as the two ends of a line, with FTP as a slider between them. The slider's position decides how the customer spread is divided between the two desks (part 3) — and therefore which behavior the bank is rewarding:

  • FTP in the middle ("FTP normal", 3% – 5% – 7% in the sketch): both desks earn a fair 2%. No side is favored; the balance sheet grows evenly.
  • FTP pushed right / higher (4% – 6% – 8%): the gap between the deposit rate and FTP widens — every deposit now earns the branch a fat margin, so deposits become more attractive to the bank and branches chase them. Meanwhile lending desks buy their funding at a higher internal price, their margin thins, and loans become less attractive to write. Net effect: the bank gathers liquidity.
  • FTP pushed left / lower (2% – 4% – 6%): the mirror image. Deposits barely pay the branch anything (less attractive deposits), while lending desks get cheap internal funding and quote aggressively (more attractive loans). Net effect: the bank deploys liquidity into assets.

How to use the lever — goal by goal

  • Bank needs liquidity (loan growth outrunning deposits, LCR under pressure, wholesale markets expensive or shut): shift FTP up. Branches launch deposit campaigns within weeks; loan pricing drifts up and demand cools. This is exactly the "liquidity-based activity" of the sketch's title.
  • Bank is drowning in liquidity (deposits pile up with nowhere to go, excess parked at the central bank at low rates): shift FTP down. Deposit-gathering stops being rewarded, lending desks get cheap funds and go hunting for assets.
  • Bank needs a specific kind of funding — say long-term money for NSFR: don't move the whole curve, raise the FTP credit at long tenors only. Branches respond by pushing 12-month+ term deposits rather than more current accounts.
  • Bank wants less of a specific product (long fixed-rate mortgages it can't hedge cheaply): raise the FTP charge on that product's tenor, and origination slows without any memo ordering it to.

Two disciplines keep this honest. First, the lever works precisely because it leaks into customer pricing — branches pass their FTP economics on to real deposit and loan rates; that leak is the mechanism, not a side effect. Second, every deliberate tilt away from the market-based curve is a strategic overlay — checklist item 6 of the main FTP doc: it must be explicit, ALCO-approved, and temporary. A silently tilted FTP curve stops measuring reality, and after a while nobody — including treasury — knows which businesses are actually profitable.
