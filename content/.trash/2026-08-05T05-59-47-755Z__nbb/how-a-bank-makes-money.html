How a Bank Makes Money

NBB research · Bank treasury series, part 1 of 4 · 2026

Strip away the branches, the apps, and the marble lobbies, and a commercial bank is a surprisingly simple machine: it borrows money from people who don't need it right now (depositors) and lends it to people who do (borrowers), keeping the difference in interest as its income. Everything else in this series — deposit pricing, funds transfer pricing (FTP), asset–liability management (ALM) — exists to protect and divide up that difference.

This is part 1 of a four-part series: (1) how a bank makes money · (2) deposit rates vs loan rates · (3) FTP · (4) ALM fundamentals.

1. A bank is a balance sheet

The clearest way to see a bank is as a balance sheet with two sides that must always be equal:

  • Assets — what the bank owns and what earns it interest: loans to customers, securities (e.g. government bonds), and some cash held for safety.
  • Liabilities & equity — where the money came from: customer deposits, wholesale funding (money borrowed from other banks or markets), and the shareholders' own capital (equity).

The balance is not an accounting nicety — it is physics. Every extra dirham/dollar of loans must be funded by an extra dirham/dollar of deposits, wholesale borrowing, or equity. When you play with the simulator below, watch how the machine automatically plugs the gap: surplus deposits get parked in securities, and a loan book bigger than the deposit base forces the bank to borrow expensively in the wholesale market.

2. The spread: net interest income

The bank pays a low rate on what it owes and charges a higher rate on what it owns. Two numbers summarize the whole business:

  • Net interest income (NII) = interest earned on assets − interest paid on liabilities. This is the raw profit engine, measured in currency.
  • Net interest margin (NIM) = NII ÷ earning assets. This is the same thing as a percentage, so banks of different sizes can be compared. A typical commercial bank runs a NIM of roughly 1.5–3.5%.

Banks also earn fees (cards, FX, advisory), but for a classic commercial bank the interest spread is the dominant source of income — which is why the rest of this series is about defending it.

Things worth trying:

  • Push loans to 140m while deposits stay at 100m. The bank must borrow 30m wholesale at the market rate — watch how much of the extra income the funding cost eats.
  • Drop loans to 40m. Now 70m sits in securities earning only the market rate. Deposits are wasted on low-yield assets and NIM sags.
  • Raise the market rate to 7% with deposits still at 1.5%. Cheap, stable deposits suddenly become enormously valuable — this is why banks fight for them. (Part 2 explains why deposit rates lag the market.)

4. The catch: the two sides don't behave the same

If the story ended here, banking would be easy. The complication is that the two sides of the balance sheet are different in kind, not just in rate:

  • Loans are for fixed terms (a 5-year car loan, a 20-year mortgage); most deposits can leave tomorrow.
  • Loan rates and deposit rates reprice at different speeds when the central bank moves.
  • The people who set loan prices (the lending business) and the people who gather deposits (the branches) are different teams — someone must split the margin between them fairly.

Managing these mismatches is the job of the bank's treasury, through a discipline called asset–liability management (ALM), and the tool it uses to split the margin is funds transfer pricing (FTP). That's the rest of this series.
